Here's something that surprises many shooters new to precision rimfire: **two boxes of the same brand and model of .22LR can shoot very differently in the same rifle.** One box prints tiny groups. The next opens up for no obvious reason.

The difference is usually the **lot number**. Understanding lots, and testing for them, is one of the cheapest and most effective ways to improve your rimfire accuracy.

## What Is an Ammo Lot?

Ammunition is made in production runs. A **lot** is a batch made with the same components, on the same equipment, in the same period. Each lot gets a number, printed on the box or the case.

Within a lot, ammo is relatively consistent. **Between lots**, small changes in priming compound, powder, bullet weight, or case dimensions can change how the ammo performs.

## Why Rimfire Is So Lot-Sensitive

Several things make .22LR especially sensitive:

- **Priming.** Rimfire primer compound is spun into the rim. Small variations in how much there is, and how evenly it's spread, affect ignition and velocity.
- **Velocity spread.** Small velocity differences change where each bullet exits during the barrel's vibration (see [What a Rimfire Tuner Does](/blog/how-rimfire-tuners-work/)).
- **Every barrel is different.** Chamber and bore dimensions vary slightly from barrel to barrel, so a lot that shoots well in one rifle may not in another.

The result: **your rifle will "prefer" some lots over others.** The only way to find out which is to test.

> **The payoff:** Many shooters find that switching to a well-matched lot shrinks groups more than any accessory upgrade, and costs much less.

## How to Test Ammo Lots: A Simple Process

### 1. Gather samples

Buy a box or two of **several different lots**, either of the same ammo or a few different match-grade options. Write each lot number on its box.

### 2. Set up for consistency

- Shoot at **50 yards** from a **solid front rest and rear bag**.
- Pick a **calm day**, or shoot early.
- **Foul the barrel** with 10 to 20 rounds of the first lot before recording groups.
- Use a fresh aiming point for each group.

### 3. Shoot multiple groups per lot

One group tells you very little. Shoot **at least three to five 5-shot groups**, or a few 10-shot groups, per lot. Fire a few fouling shots each time you switch lots so the barrel adjusts.

### 4. Measure and record

For each lot, record:

- Lot number
- Date, temperature, and conditions
- Each group size, especially **vertical** spread
- The average group size

### 5. Compare averages, not best groups

The lot that shot one tiny group isn't necessarily the winner. Look for the lot with the **smallest, most consistent average**. Consistency wins matches.

## Found a Good Lot? Buy Deep.

When you find a lot your rifle likes, **buy as much of it as you can afford**: a case or more. Once it's gone, you start testing again.

Store it properly. Keep it in a cool, dry place with stable temperatures, since heat and humidity swings can affect performance over time.

## Testing Tips

- **Tune and test together, carefully.** If your rifle has a tuner, test lots at one fixed tuner setting first, then fine-tune the tuner for your chosen lot.
- **Use a chronograph if you have one.** Lower velocity spread often goes with better accuracy at distance.
- **Don't test on a windy day.** Wind will hide the differences you're looking for.
- **Keep good notes.** A simple spreadsheet becomes very valuable over time.
- **Ask about test centers.** Some ammunition makers and dealers offer lot testing services, where your rifle is shot with many lots in a controlled tunnel. This can save time if you're serious about benchrest.

## The Bottom Line

Your rifle, barrel, and tuner can only perform as well as the ammunition you feed them. Lot testing takes a range session or two, but finding the right lot is one of the biggest accuracy improvements you can make.
